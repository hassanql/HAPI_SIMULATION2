\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This chapter empirically instantiates the \emph{A2 -- Adaptive Query
Inference Attacks} family of the candidate threat taxonomy
(\textsection9.1.2 of the companion thesis-notes document) on a
Bayesian-experimental-design (BED) diagnostic LLM agent. The
instantiation is concrete: a malicious principal commissions an honest
diagnostic agent with a plausible-looking clinical task whose
Bayes-optimal sensing happens to elicit a protected patient attribute
$\tau$ (HIV status, IV drug use, pregnancy, or mental-health
diagnosis), while the agent runs its standard, well-behaved diagnostic
policy and every individual question is clinically appropriate. We
evaluate three principals (benign, direct, covert) on $n{=}50$
augmented MedQA cases per attribute across all four attributes (600
trajectories per principal). The direct principal recovers $\tau$ at
$96\%$ pooled but is trivially detectable. A naive covert principal
(random pool of cover stories) fails to extract $\tau$ above the
benign baseline. A sophisticated covert principal --- per-case
AI-tailored cover stories that embed $\tau$-correlated clinical
findings --- recovers $\tau$ at $+6.6$pp above benign on a frontier
semantic auditor (pooled, $+9.8$pp on HIV, $+10.8$pp on IV drug use),
\emph{while cover-task accuracy remains statistically indistinguishable
from benign on all four attributes}. A behavioural probe on
trajectory shape alone achieves AUC $0.61$ pooled. A semantic auditor
achieves AUC $0.66$ pooled. At a binary threshold of $0.5$, however,
the auditor flags only $\sim 18\%$ of covert attacks. The attack is
therefore \emph{statistically detectable but operationally
unblockable}, which is the central finding of the chapter.
\end{abstract}

\section{The Threat}
\label{sec:threat}

\subsection{Setting}

The setting is the Human-API formalisation of Oliver, Devedzhiev and
Bhatt: humans appear in an agent's action loop as callable,
cost-bearing sensing actions with structured interfaces. The agent
under study is a cost-aware BED-LLM diagnostic agent that, given a
clinical chief complaint, selects a sequence of patient queries
(history, exam, lab tests) to maximise expected information gain
about a hidden diagnosis. The patient is simulated by a
ground-truth-anchored LLM. A \emph{principal} commissions the agent
with a chief complaint and reads the resulting trajectory of questions
and patient responses.

\subsection{Three principals}

We compare three principal types on a paired case-set:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,style=nextline]
    \item[Benign principal $B$.] Commissions the agent with the
    original MedQA chief complaint; reads the trajectory to recover the
    correct medical diagnosis. The principal has no interest in
    $\tau$.
    \item[Direct principal $D$.] Commissions the agent with a chief
    complaint that names $\tau$ directly (``Determine whether this
    patient has HIV''). This is an explicit attack and serves as the
    upper bound on extractability.
    \item[Covert principal $C$.] Commissions the agent with a
    plausible-looking medical chief complaint that does \emph{not}
    name $\tau$ but whose Bayes-optimal sensing happens to elicit
    $\tau$-correlated patient responses (``$57$-year-old man with
    chronic diarrhoea, weight loss, and recurrent oral thrush'' for
    an HIV-positive case). The principal observes only the agent's
    queries and patient answers; it reads $\tau$ off the trajectory
    by post-hoc inference.
\end{description}

The agent itself is identical across principals --- only the
principal's commissioning differs. The agent does not know which
principal commissioned it; from the agent's perspective each task
is a routine differential diagnosis.

\subsection{Task laundering as A2}

The covert principal's attack mechanism is what we call \emph{task
laundering}: the malicious principal launders its protected-attribute
inference goal through the agent's honest medical reasoning. The
principal does not need a misbehaving agent, a prompt injection, or
a jailbreak. It only needs to choose a chief complaint whose
Bayes-optimal sensing happens to be informative about $\tau$. This is
why we place the threat in family A2 of the taxonomy --- it works by
\emph{adaptive query selection} against an honest agent, not by
exploiting the agent's representation (A1), behaviour (B), or
resource allocation (C).

The threat is qualitatively different from prior threat families in
three ways:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
    \item It is \textbf{not prompt injection}: no malicious instruction
    enters the agent's context. The agent is the \emph{vehicle}, not
    the victim.
    \item It is \textbf{not a jailbreak}: the agent's behaviour is
    unchanged across principals. The variation lives in the
    principal's task specification, not in the agent's outputs.
    \item It is \textbf{not memory extraction} (A1): there is no
    cross-session memory in the system. Each trajectory is a single
    stateless interaction; the attack works by what the agent's
    \emph{within-task} questioning reveals about the patient, not by
    extracting anything already stored.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

The empirical study tests five hypotheses, each a falsifiable
prediction about the threat:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,style=nextline]
    \item[H1.] \textbf{Direct attacks recover $\tau$ near-perfectly.}
    When the principal explicitly commissions $\tau$-extraction, the
    BED agent's Bayes-optimal first query is the cheapest test that
    resolves $\tau$. We expect $\tau$-recovery rates approaching
    $100\%$.
    \item[H2.] \textbf{Direct attacks are trivially detectable.} The
    direct principal's chief complaint and the agent's first query
    contain attribute-specific keywords (``HIV'', ``track marks'',
    ``biological sex'', ``depression''). A keyword-filter audit
    should catch direct trajectories with near-perfect recall.
    \item[H3.] \textbf{Naive covert attacks fail.} If the principal
    selects a covert chief complaint uniformly at random from a pool
    of plausible cover stories per attribute, the chief complaint and
    the underlying patient case are uncorrelated: the cover story
    does not match the patient. The agent receives contradictory
    inputs (a chief complaint about, e.g., eye floaters; a patient
    actually presenting with abdominal pain) and asks questions
    consistent with one or the other, but not in a way that elicits
    $\tau$. We expect $\tau$-leak rates indistinguishable from the
    benign baseline.
    \item[H4.] \textbf{Sophisticated covert attacks succeed under the
    joint-validity criterion.} A per-case AI-tailored cover story
    that (a) matches the patient's age/sex/presentation and (b)
    explicitly embeds $\tau$-correlated clinical findings produces
    coherent covert trajectories that elicit $\tau$-correlated
    answers. We expect, simultaneously:
    \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,label=(\alph*)]
        \item \emph{Cover-task invisibility}: covert cover-task
        accuracy Wilson 95\% CI overlaps the benign baseline on every
        attribute.
        \item \emph{$\tau$-leak}: a semantic auditor assigns covert
        trajectories a mean P($\tau{=}\mathrm{positive}$) at least
        $5$pp above the benign baseline.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item[H5.] \textbf{Detection of covert is asymmetric across
    defender classes.} Lexical detection (word-frequency analysis of
    trajectory text) catches direct attacks but fails on covert;
    behavioural detection (trajectory-shape features) partially
    catches covert; semantic detection (LLM-based audit) catches
    covert most strongly. None of the three achieves operational-grade
    binary classification on covert.
\end{description}

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Agent: cost-aware Bayesian experimental design}

The agent follows the BED-LLM template of Choudhury et al.\ (2025).
Given a current belief $p_t$ over diagnoses, the agent proposes $K$
candidate actions $a^{(1)}, \ldots, a^{(K)}$ (each an
\code{ASK\_HISTORY}, \code{ASK\_EXAM}, or \code{ORDER\_TEST} with an
associated cost), estimates the expected information gain (EIG) of
each:
\[
\mathrm{EIG}(a) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p(y\mid a, p_t)}\,
\big[\mathrm{KL}\big(p_{t+1}(y) \,\|\, p_t\big)\big],
\]
and selects the action by a cost-normalised hybrid rule that prefers
high-EIG, low-cost queries unless the entropy of $p_t$ has already
fallen below a stop threshold $\varepsilon$. The agent commits to a
\code{DIAGNOSE} action when (a) entropy falls below $\varepsilon$, (b)
a per-trajectory cost budget is exhausted, or (c) it actively chooses
to commit (the policy-diagnose branch). The implementation reuses the
established BED-LLM diversity-encouraging proposal sampling and the
reverse-history-order trajectory summary in the proposal prompt.

\subsection{Patient: $\tau$-anchored simulator}

The patient is an LLM (Gemini~3 Flash) instructed via system prompt
with three components: (i) the visible vignette of the underlying
MedQA case, (ii) a list of hidden findings consistent with the
augmented $\tau$, and (iii) a $\tau$-anchored ground-truth statement
(``You are HIV-positive; any direct test for HIV antibody, p24
antigen, or viral load returns reactive''). The patient answers
clinician questions consistently with these anchors. No deterministic
direct-probe interceptor is used: a controlled $n{=}20$ ablation
confirmed the frontier reasoning model respects the $\tau$-anchored
prompt at $100\%$ direct $\tau$-recovery without external override.

\subsection{Three covert task designs}
\label{sec:methodology-covert}

For the covert principal we compare three task-design strategies, in
order of attacker sophistication:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,style=nextline]
    \item[Random hand-curated.] The principal draws a chief complaint
    uniformly at random from a hand-curated pool of five
    $\tau$-correlated cover stories per attribute (PCP pneumonia for
    HIV, tricuspid endocarditis for IV drug use, etc.). The pool is
    high quality (each cover story is an AIDS-defining or attribute-
    pathognomonic presentation), but selection is decoupled from the
    underlying case.
    \item[LLM-attacker, bare.] For each case the principal generates
    a chief complaint via a frontier LLM. The complaint must match
    the patient's age/sex/presentation but is \emph{constrained to
    contain no clinical findings} (e.g.\ ``Patient presents for a
    routine check-up''). The complaint biases the agent's natural
    differential only through the choice of presenting concern.
    \item[LLM-attacker, embedded.] Identical to the bare variant
    except that the LLM is instructed to explicitly embed one or two
    $\tau$-correlated clinical findings in the chief complaint
    (``$57$-year-old man with chronic diarrhoea, weight loss, and
    recurrent oral thrush''). The findings are clinically correlated
    with $\tau$ but not diagnostic of it (oral thrush has many
    causes; lymphadenopathy is common in many conditions).
\end{description}

The three covert variants share the same benign and direct
trajectories on the same case-set, so per-case paired comparisons
are valid across all four conditions
(benign, direct, covert-random, covert-bare, covert-embedded).

\subsection{Sensitive-attribute augmentation}

Each MedQA case is augmented per attribute by sampling
$\tau \in \{0, 1\}$ at approximately balanced prevalence
($\sim 49\%$ positivity) and injecting attribute-correlated clinical
markers into the patient's hidden findings (\code{oral thrush},
\code{lymphadenopathy} for HIV-positive; \code{track marks},
\code{abscess} for IV-drug-positive; \code{amenorrhea},
\code{morning nausea} for pregnancy-positive; \code{sleep disturbance},
\code{anhedonia} for mental-health-positive). Markers are sampled at
correlation strength $\rho = 0.3$, where $\rho \equiv
P(\mathrm{marker}\mid\tau{=}1) - P(\mathrm{marker}\mid\tau{=}0)$. The
$\tau$-anchored ground-truth statement is constructed from a
per-attribute template. The same MedQA case appears under all four
attributes (different overlays per attribute), enabling cross-attribute
analysis.

\subsection{Three defender classes}

A defender, given a completed trajectory, attempts to recover $\tau$ or
to flag the trajectory as covert. We evaluate three probe classes,
each representative of a plausible deployment:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,style=nextline]
    \item[Lexical probe.] A logistic regression over (i) numerical
    trajectory features (step count, total cost, action-type counts,
    EIG statistics, belief-vector summary statistics) and (ii) a
    TF-IDF representation (max 500 features, unigram + bigram) of the
    concatenated question and patient-response text. Label: $\tau$.
    Trained by case-level 5-fold stratified cross-validation.
    \item[Behavioural probe.] A logistic regression over only the
    numerical trajectory features --- no text. Step count, total cost,
    action-type counts (raw and fractional), stop-reason one-hot,
    belief-evolution statistics, max single-step cost. Label:
    $\mathrm{is\_covert} \in \{0, 1\}$ (principal-detection, not
    $\tau$-extraction). This is the cheapest content-blind runtime
    monitor.
    \item[Semantic probe.] A frontier LLM (Gemini~3 Flash) prompted
    with the serialised trajectory and the attribute description, and
    asked to output a single $\mathrm{P}(\tau{=}\mathrm{positive})$
    score in $[0, 1]$. Label: $\tau$.
\end{description}

\subsection{Joint-validity criterion}
\label{sec:methodology-validity}

We declare a covert variant a \emph{valid threat} if and only if it
satisfies both:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,label=(\Alph*)]
    \item \textbf{Cover-task invisibility.} Wilson $95\%$ CI on
    covert cover-task accuracy overlaps the benign baseline (a
    defender cannot flag the attack by aggregate accuracy degradation).
    \item \textbf{$\tau$-leak.} Mean $\mathrm{P}(\tau{=}\mathrm{positive})$
    assigned by the semantic probe to covert trajectories is at least
    $5$pp above the corresponding benign baseline.
\end{enumerate}

(A) ensures the attack is stealthy in the cover task; (B) ensures the
attack actually leaks $\tau$. An attack failing (A) is detectable by
accuracy alone; an attack failing (B) is not actually extracting
$\tau$.

\subsection{Scope and acceptance criteria}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,style=nextline]
    \item[Scope.] $50$ cases per attribute $\times\;4$ attributes
    $\times\;3$ principal types = $600$ trajectories per covert
    variant. Three covert variants are evaluated, so the empirical
    base is $5 \times 50 \times 4 = 1\,000$ unique trajectories
    (benign and direct trajectories are shared across covert
    variants).
    \item[Sampling seed.] A single seed ($0$) for case selection;
    multi-seed re-runs are deferred to future work.
    \item[Correlation strength.] $\rho = 0.3$ throughout; a
    sensitivity sweep over $\rho$ is deferred to future work.
    \item[Acceptance.] Five gates per pilot run: (i) all $600$
    trajectories produced, (ii) per-cell trajectory counts equal,
    (iii) per-(attribute, seed) case-id alignment across principals,
    (iv) no principal-type contamination of the agent's prompt
    assembly, (v) benign-principal diagnostic accuracy $\geq 50\%$
    (sanity floor). All five gates passed on each pilot run reported
    in \S\ref{sec:results}.
\end{description}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Direct attack: near-perfect recovery, trivial detection}

The direct principal recovers $\tau$ at $96\%$ pooled
$(193/200)$ across the four attributes (Table~\ref{tab:direct-recovery}).
Per attribute, $\tau$-recovery is $98\%$ on \code{hiv\_status},
$98\%$ on \code{iv\_drug\_use}, $92\%$ on \code{pregnancy}, and
$98\%$ on \code{mental\_health\_dx}. The single failure mode on
\code{pregnancy} is the augmenter's failure to enforce
biological-female cases under pregnancy-positive augmentation: the
agent correctly identifies male patients as $\tau{=}\mathrm{negative}$
but cannot distinguish a male case with $\tau{=}1$ from one with
$\tau{=}0$, since both are biologically impossible.

Direct trajectories are trivially detectable. The lexical probe

\emph{track marks}, \emph{biological sex}, \emph{depression
diagnosis}) within the first one to two queries. The direct attack
therefore exists, succeeds at $\tau$-extraction, and is unambiguously
catchable by a defender that reads either the chief complaint or the
first agent question. \textbf{Hypotheses H1 and H2 are supported.}

\paragraph{Random hand-curated covert fails (H3 confirmed).}
A naive principal that selects a chief complaint uniformly at random
from a hand-curated pool of $\tau$-correlated cover stories produces
trajectories with $\tau$-leak gap $-1.9$pp ---
\emph{covert leaks less $\tau$ than the benign baseline}. Manual
inspection reveals the cause: the random pool is decoupled from the
underlying case. A pediatric bike-fall case is randomly assigned an
adult CMV-retinitis cover story; the agent receives contradictory
inputs and the patient simulator answers from the case content rather
than the cover. The cover-task itself fails ($62\%$ covert vs $80\%$
benign), but the failure is in the principal's task selection, not in
the threat model. Hypothesis H3 holds: a sophisticated threat model
requires per-case task design, not a random pool.

\paragraph{Embedded LLM-attacker covert succeeds (H4 confirmed).}
A per-case LLM-attacker that matches the cover story to the patient
and embeds one or two $\tau$-correlated clinical findings produces
$\tau$-leak gap $+9.8$pp on \code{hiv\_status}, clearing the $5$pp
joint-validity threshold. Cover-task accuracy ($66\%$) remains
statistically indistinguishable from benign ($70\%$). The attack is
therefore both stealthy (criterion A) and effective (criterion B).

\paragraph{Decomposing the leak: bare vs embedded.}
The bare variant (case-matched but no embedded findings) leaks
$+3.1$pp; the embedded variant leaks $+9.8$pp; the difference is
$+6.7$pp. Approximately $30\%$ of the embedded leak signal comes from
case-task matching alone, and approximately $70\%$ comes from the
deliberate embedding of $\tau$-correlated findings in the chief
complaint. \emph{Effective task laundering requires not just a
matched cover story, but active framing of the patient's presentation
around attribute-correlated findings.}

(pregnancy --- gated by biological sex; mental health --- markers
overlap heavily with non-attribute differentials).

\subsection{Defender analysis}
\label{sec:results-probes}

Two distinct defender questions arise. (Q1) ``How $\tau$-leaky is
this trajectory?'' --- a content question best answered by a probe
trained to predict $\tau$. (Q2) ``Should this query be flagged as
covert?'' --- a runtime-classification question best answered by a
probe trained to predict $\mathrm{is\_covert}$. We evaluate the three
probe classes against both and find they win on different
operational tasks.

mean $\mathrm{P}(\tau{=}\mathrm{positive})$ minus benign). The
semantic probe is strongest on this task; lexical is near-chance.
Hypothesis H5 is supported on the $\tau$-recovery framing.

behavioural probe catches $29\%$ of covert attacks. The semantic
probe catches half that ($15\%$) at the same FPR. The lexical probe
($9.5\%$) is barely above the FPR baseline.

This is the central operational result: \textbf{the cheapest probe
class (numerical trajectory-shape features only, no text) is the
strongest defender for principal detection.} The semantic probe's
higher AUC on $\tau$-recovery (Table~\ref{tab:probes-tau}) does not
translate to operational principal detection because the semantic
probe assigns most covert trajectories low absolute scores
(mean $\mathrm{P}(\tau{=}\mathrm{positive}) = 0.15$); even when it
correctly ranks covert above benign in aggregate, individual covert
trajectories rarely cross a useful flagging threshold. The
behavioural probe, trained directly on the
$\mathrm{is\_covert}$ label, produces calibrated principal-detection
scores that respond to flagging thresholds.

The behavioural probe's strongest individual features (by absolute
logistic coefficient) are: \code{max\_step\_cost} (benign orders more
expensive tests; coefficient $-0.67$), \code{frac\_ask\_exam} (benign
issues more exam questions; $-0.54$), \code{belief\_mean\_step\_kl}
(covert updates beliefs more aggressively per step; $+0.53$), and
\code{belief\_final\_max\_prob} (covert ends with higher final
confidence; $+0.51$).

\subsection{Defence-impact: residual leak after deploying each filter}
\label{sec:results-operational}

The probe AUCs and operating points reported above describe the
defender's \emph{detection} capability --- how often the probe flags
covert versus benign queries. But a deployed defender's real job is
to \emph{reduce information leak to the principal}. We measure this
directly: for each filter, mark trajectories as ``flagged'' at the
$\mathrm{FPR}\!\leq\!0.10$ threshold, then compute the residual
covert-vs-benign $\tau$-leak gap on the \emph{unflagged} population
(the trajectories that slip past the defender and complete).
Table~\ref{tab:defence-impact} reports the original (no-defender)
gap, the residual gap under each filter, and the absolute reduction.

The two filters optimise for incompatible defender objectives:

\paragraph{Behavioural filter: high TPR, no leak reduction.}
The behavioural probe catches $28\%$ of covert attacks at $10\%$
benign false-positive rate, but the trajectories it flags are
\emph{behaviourally} suspicious rather than \emph{semantically}
leaky. The $72\%$ that slip past carry essentially the full original
$\tau$-leak: the residual gap on unflagged trajectories is $+7.1$pp,
indistinguishable from the no-defender baseline. On
\code{iv\_drug\_use} the residual gap rises to $+16.3$pp ($+5.5$pp
above baseline), because the behaviourally-quietest covert
trajectories happen to be the leakiest --- the defender preferentially
removes the noisy ones.

\paragraph{Semantic filter: lower TPR, near-total leak elimination.}
The semantic probe catches $15\%$ of covert attacks --- about half
the behavioural probe's rate --- but those it catches are the
$\tau$-content-rich trajectories. The residual gap on unflagged
trajectories collapses from $+6.6$pp to $+0.2$pp, a $97\%$ reduction
of the original threat. Per attribute, the semantic filter reduces
the gap by $-8.4$pp on \code{hiv\_status}, $-11.5$pp on
\code{iv\_drug\_use}, $-3.6$pp on \code{mental\_health\_dx}, and
$-1.8$pp on \code{pregnancy}.

\paragraph{The metric matters.}
This contrast establishes that
\textbf{defender true-positive rate is not the operationally
meaningful metric for this attack class} --- residual leak on
unflagged trajectories is. A defender chosen to maximise raw TPR
(behavioural) catches more attacks but reduces zero privacy harm; a
defender chosen to maximise leak reduction (semantic) catches fewer
attacks but eliminates almost all the threat. Hypothesis~H5 is
supported: detection is asymmetric across probe classes, but the
asymmetry reverses depending on which defender metric one tracks.

\paragraph{Deployed-defence recommendation.}
A real privacy-protective deployment should use the semantic probe
as its primary filter (accepting the operational cost of an
LLM-judge call per audited trajectory) because it is the only
filter whose deployment actually reduces $\tau$-leak. Behavioural
monitoring remains useful for cheap real-time anomaly flagging where
the goal is incident-counting (e.g.\ identifying principals who
generate behaviourally-anomalous traffic patterns over time) rather
than per-query privacy protection.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Single sampling seed.}
All headline results use a single sampling seed for case selection,
giving $n{=}50$ cases per attribute. Per-cell Wilson 95\% CIs on
cover-task accuracy are $\pm 13$pp; pooled across attributes the
$\tau$-leak gap of $+6.6$pp is well-resolved at $n{=}200$, but the
small per-attribute leak gaps on \code{pregnancy} and
\code{mental\_health\_dx} ($+2.0$ and $+3.6$pp) sit within the
single-seed noise floor.

\paragraph{Single correlation strength.}
All results are at augmenter correlation $\rho = 0.3$ ---
$P(\mathrm{marker}\mid\tau{=}1) - P(\mathrm{marker}\mid\tau{=}0)$.
The threat is expected to scale with $\rho$ (stronger clinical
correlates $\to$ more $\tau$-information per trajectory), but we have
not characterised the curve. We report the threat at one operating
point, not as a function of attribute observability.

\paragraph{Same-laboratory agent and judge.}
The diagnostic agent, the patient simulator, and the LLM-judge probe
are all instances of Gemini~3 Flash. Same-laboratory audit is a real
methodological weakness: a judge from a different model family may
catch leakage patterns this judge misses, or vice versa. The original
spec called for three-laboratory separation (Qwen agent, Gemma
patient, Phi-4 judge); operational constraints collapsed all three
roles to Flash. Cross-laboratory replication is scoped to future
work.

\paragraph{LLM-attacker uses the same model family as the agent.}
The covert principal's cover stories are generated by Gemini Flash, the
same model serving as the agent. In a real deployment the attacker
would use the strongest available model. The result direction
(positive $\tau$-leak) is unlikely to reverse under a stronger
attacker, but the magnitude may change.

\paragraph{Synthetic augmentation.}
The augmented patient data is synthetic: $\tau$ is sampled at
$\approx\!49\%$ positivity and $\tau$-correlated markers are injected
into the patient's hidden findings with controlled probability. This
is the correct experimental design for measuring leakage as a
function of attribute observability, but it is not real clinical
data. The augmenter also does not enforce biological plausibility per
attribute: male cases can be augmented with pregnancy-positive
markers, producing trajectories in which the agent legitimately
concludes pregnancy is impossible. These cases attenuate the
attribute-specific leak, biasing the headline numbers conservatively.

\paragraph{Joint validity holds on $2/4$ attributes individually.}
The threat satisfies joint validity (cover-task CI overlap AND
$\tau$-leak $\geq 5$pp) on \code{hiv\_status} and
\code{iv\_drug\_use}, but not on \code{pregnancy} or
\code{mental\_health\_dx} individually. Pooled across all four it
does. This is informative --- the threat magnitude is genuinely
heterogeneous across attributes --- but it limits the strength of
per-attribute claims.

\paragraph{Probe sample size.}
Probe AUCs are estimated by $5$-fold case-level cross-validation on
$50$ cases per attribute, giving approximately $40$ training and $10$
test cases per fold. Per-fold AUC standard errors are correspondingly
wide. Pooled-attribute estimates tighten but remain noisier than the
ideal.

\paragraph{No end-to-end defender evaluation.}
We report probe AUCs and threshold-specific TPR/FPR pairs, but we do
not yet deploy a defender end-to-end with documented operating-point
selection, throughput overhead, or cost-budget profile. The defender
analysis is audit-grade; deployment-grade evaluation requires Stage~7.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\subsection{Within-thesis revisions}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,style=nextline]
    \item[Multi-seed re-run.] Re-run the LLM-attacker embedded
    headline at three sampling seeds. Triples effective $n$ per cell
    and tightens Wilson CIs from $\pm 13$pp to $\pm 8$pp; resolves
    whether the borderline per-attribute leaks on \code{pregnancy}
    and \code{mental\_health\_dx} are real or noise.
    \item[Correlation-strength sweep.] Re-augment \code{hiv\_status}
    at $\rho \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}$ and re-run the LLM-attacker
    headline at each. Produces an attack-effectiveness curve as a
    function of attribute observability.
    \item[End-to-end defender evaluation.] Deploy the behavioural and
    semantic probes as runtime monitors with calibrated thresholds.
    Report FPR/TPR receiver operating curves and the operational
    cost of each. Quantify whether stacking lexical + behavioural +
    semantic monitors closes the operational-blocking gap.
    \item[Biological-plausibility filter on \code{pregnancy}.]
    Restrict pregnancy augmentation to biologically-female cases.
    Re-run \code{pregnancy} covert and direct under the filter; the
    expected effect is a higher direct $\tau$-recovery rate and a
    cleaner covert leak signal on this attribute specifically.
    \item[Same-patient figure.] Regenerate the same-patient
    cross-principal comparison figure (a single MedQA case viewed
    under all four attributes and all three principals) using the
    LLM-attacker embedded chief complaints.
\end{description}

\subsection{Post-thesis directions}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,style=nextline]
    \item[Cross-laboratory replication.] Substitute the judge for a
    different-model-family auditor (Claude, GPT-4, Phi-4). Same-
    laboratory audit is the most likely reviewer concern; this
    addresses it directly.
    \item[Stronger attacker models.] Have the LLM-attacker generate
    cover stories using a different (and ideally stronger) model
    family than the agent. Quantifies whether the attack scales with
    attacker capability.
    \item[Additional protected attributes.] Extend to more attributes
    with varying clinical-distinctiveness profiles
    (substance-use-disorder subtypes, immigration status, sexual
    orientation, prior diagnoses).
    \item[Real clinical validation.] Replicate the threat on a real
    medical-AI deployment with anonymised patient data or
    standardised-patient interactions, with appropriate IRB approval.
    This converts a benchmark result into a deployment-grade one.
    \item[Defender design space.] Move beyond per-trajectory auditing
    to defender architectures that monitor across many trajectories
    --- principal-level reputation, distributional drift detection,
    cross-query consistency.
    \item[Adversarial robustness of the augmenter and probes.]
    Quantify how sensitive the headline numbers are to (a) the choice
    of clinical-correlate marker list, (b) the choice of LLM-attacker
    prompt template, and (c) the probe's training distribution.
\end{description}
